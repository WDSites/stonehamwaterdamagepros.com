Central Square, Stoneham

Water Damage Cleanup in Central Square, Stoneham, MA

Older commercial blocks and mixed-use buildings around the Common share walls, floors and plumbing, so water rarely stays in one place.

Call (339) 333-9345

Central Square is the center of Stoneham, where Main Street, Center Street and Central Street come together near the Common. The National Register listing for the Central Square Historic District describes it as the town's central business district and its largest concentration of nineteenth and early twentieth century commercial buildings. Water damage in a building like that behaves differently from a single house, and this page explains why.

If water is moving now, call (339) 333-9345 and say which floor and which units are affected.

What the district looks like

The National Register record says the district is roughly bounded by Main, Central, Church, Winter and Common Streets. It was listed in January 1990. Landmarks it names include the 1858 Italianate factory building at 426 Main Street, the 1904 public library, three Second Empire commercial blocks built between 1868 and 1890, the 1918 central fire station and a town hall dating from 1939. Churches mark the northern edge near Common Street.

Main Street is Route 28, which began as the Andover and Medford Turnpike. Buildings of that age were put up in different decades and altered many times. That matters for water, because pipes, floors and ceilings in one building often come from several eras.

Why water travels in older mixed-use buildings

In a single house, a leak usually wets one room and the one below. In a block with shops, offices or apartments in the same structure, water can move through shared floors, walls and chases into units owned or leased by other people. This page cannot say how any particular building is used, so ask your property manager or landlord how your space connects to its neighbors.

Three things help when water starts in or near your space. Find out who controls the shut-off valve for your unit. Tell neighbors above and below as soon as you can. Write down who is responsible for the building and who holds the policy. Those answers affect who calls whom.

Safety first: the first hour

Stay out of any space where water is near panels, outlets or lighting. Do not switch breakers on while standing in water. For sparking, a burning smell or a gas odor, leave and call 911 or the utility. If the building has an alarm or sprinkler system, leave it to the property owner and fire department.

Shut off the water to your unit if you can do it safely, and tell the building owner right away. Keep people and pets out of standing water, which can hide electrical danger or carry contamination. Photograph what you see and move inventory, paper and electronics to a dry spot only if the area is safe to enter.

A pipe or fixture upstairs

Water from a floor above can reach ceilings, light fixtures and the stock beneath them. The commercial water damage page explains what extraction and drying look like in a business space.

Drains and sewer lines

A backup that surfaces in a basement or ground floor needs different handling from clean water. See sewage backup cleanup before touching anything.

Rain at the doors and windows

Storm water can come under doors and through old openings. The storm water damage page covers what to do after wind-driven rain.

What the town's records say about water

Stoneham's hazard mitigation plan lists Main Street among its locally identified flood areas, naming drainage near Carroll Street. It also records an April 16, 2018 flood that left Elm Street impassable at Central Street. The plan does not place the Carroll Street drainage inside the Square, and it also says stormwater flooding can occur anywhere in town.

Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status not verified. Check any building address on the FEMA flood map before relying on a guess. Town Public Works figures show about 75 miles of sewer line and five pumping stations, so a backup can start in a building's own line or in the main.

How cleanup and drying proceed

Restoration professionals extract standing water first, then map moisture with meters and a thermal imaging camera. Wet insulation, ceiling tile, carpet and swollen drywall that cannot be saved are removed. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers then dry floors, framing and cavities, with readings logged each visit. For the science behind the method, read structural drying.

In an older building, flooring, plaster and masonry can hold water for days after the surface looks dry. A plan should name the materials that were measured, rather than just the ones that look wet.

Insurance and records

A building owner and a tenant may each have their own policy, and which one responds depends on the cause, the lease and the policy terms. Read the policy and ask the insurer. Photos, an inventory of what was damaged, moisture readings and a written scope can all help describe the loss. Nothing on this page promises coverage or approval.

What affects the cost

Size of the wet area, floor and ceiling materials, water category, drying days, equipment and rebuild work all affect price. Shared walls can widen the scope. A written scope and price before work begins is a reasonable request. When comparing providers, ANSI/IICRC S500 is the industry's published reference for this work.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not assume the neighbor's leak is not your problem. Do not leave wet ceiling tile or carpet in place to see if it dries. Do not use a household vacuum on standing water or on anything that came from a drain. Do not discard damaged items before photographing them.

Frequently asked questions

What if the water started in a neighboring unit?

Document your side, tell the building owner or manager and keep the record of when you called. Cleanup in shared structures often involves more than one party, so ask who is responsible for the common walls and floors.

Does the Central Square Historic District affect repairs?

Listing in the National Register describes the buildings. It does not tell you which local approvals apply to your work. Local approvals are a question for the town's building office, on the garden level at 35 Central Street, which takes permit applications online and schedules inspections by phone.

Is Central Square in a FEMA flood zone?

That has not been verified for the district as a whole. Maps apply to individual addresses, so look up each building and ask your insurer how flood and plumbing losses are treated, since many policies split them.

Can a business keep operating during cleanup?

Sometimes part of a space can stay open while another part is dried, but it depends on safety, power and contamination. Ask for a written plan that says which areas are affected and for how long.

How is water damage in an apartment above a store handled?

The same steps apply, but access and responsibility matter more. Stop the source if it is yours, photograph the damage and notify the owner. Restoration professionals then dry each affected level and report readings.
