Storm Water Damage

Emergency Storm Water Damage Restoration in Stoneham, MA

Wind-driven rain, tree strikes and sump pump failures put water inside the house fast. Here is what to do in the first hours.

Call (339) 333-9345

After a big storm, the phone rings from two kinds of homes. One has a window that let in a sheet of rain. The other has a basement pump that quit when the power went out. Both end up with wet walls and floors, and both have a short window before materials swell and stain.

This page covers storm water that gets into the structure from above or the sides. Water rising from the ground and across the yard is a different problem, handled under flood damage restoration. If a leaking roof is the main issue, see roof leak water damage.

How storms put water inside a house

Wind pushes rain sideways, and that finds gaps that stay dry in ordinary weather. Window and door frames, worn caulk, loose siding, failed flashing around a bump-out and gaps around vents are the usual routes. Water enters the wall cavity first, then shows up as a stain on the drywall, a damp baseboard or a puddle at the sill.

Falling limbs add a second route. A tree strike can open the roof or siding in a moment, and rain follows. The town's hazard mitigation plan flags wind-felled trees and power lines, and singles out overhanging trees that damage lines as a known problem. If a branch is resting near your service line, that is a reason to stay away and call the utility, not to cut or move it.

Power loss adds the third. Sump pumps run on household current, so an outage can stop the pump at the wrong time and water then collects at the low point.

Spreading water: what to do right now

Storm damage often leaves several problems at once. Work through them in this order:

  1. Get everyone to safe, dry ground

    Leave any room where water is near outlets, cords, or a power panel. Do not wade through standing water.

  2. Report hazards first

    Downed lines, a gas smell or sparking outlets go to the utility or 911 before anyone else.

  3. Stop more water getting in

    If you can do it from inside, move furniture away from the opening and lay towels or plastic below it. Do not climb onto a wet roof or ladder in wind.

  4. Photograph the damage

    Take wide and close shots of the opening, the wet floors and the tree or debris, plus the weather conditions.

  5. Call the restoration line

    Describe where the water came from and what is wet. A crew can be arranged for extraction and drying.

Safety first: storms and electricity

Stay out of a wet room if the lights are flickering, an outlet is damp or the panel is in a flooded area. Shut off the main breaker only when you can get to the panel on dry footing. Otherwise leave it and phone the utility or 911. Treat every downed or sagging wire as live, even if the house lights are out.

Never run a generator indoors or in a garage, because carbon monoxide builds fast. If a sump pump is submerged, do not touch it or its cord. Water that came across a yard or through a drain may carry contamination, so avoid skin contact until a crew has looked at it.

What Stoneham's own record shows

Stoneham's hazard mitigation plan lists flood events that hit the town: June 24, 2008, when two cars were submerged on Maple Street, and March 29, 2010, when basements flooded. It also lists April 16, 2018, when Elm Street at Central Street was impassable, and August 12, 2018, when Woodland Road flooded.

The plan says stormwater flooding can happen anywhere in town and ties it to undersized culverts and insufficient detention and drainage. Locally identified flood areas include Main Street, the Franklin Street area, Dike's Pond and Montvale Avenue.

The practical takeaway is simple. Do not assume your street is spared because it is not on the list. Look up your address on the FEMA flood map and find out from your insurer what the policy does and does not pay for. FEMA-1895-DR, the severe storms and flooding declaration beginning in March 2010, designated Middlesex County for Individual Assistance. It is a reminder that storms of that size reach this part of Massachusetts.

Our process after storm water gets in

Extraction comes first. Truck-mounted or portable units draw standing water out of floors and carpet, and a small submersible pump handles a basement low spot. Moisture meters then check walls, baseboards and subfloor, and a thermal imaging camera shows wet areas behind finished surfaces.

Wet insulation, soaked pad and swollen drywall are usually cut out. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers dry what remains, and daily readings show progress.

Clean rain through a window makes for a simpler job. Water that came across the ground or up from a drain is treated as contaminated, so porous materials come out and antimicrobial treatment and HEPA air scrubbers are used. For the standing water itself, see emergency water removal.

After the storm checklist

Walk the house in daylight. Check ceilings and window corners for new stains, look at the basement floor and sump pit, and scan the siding and roof edge from the ground. Take photos of anything odd. Clear gutters and downspouts before the next storm, and test the sump pump with a bucket of water. A battery backup for the pump is a plumber's or electrician's job, not ours.

What changes the cost

The scope and price are laid out before any work starts. What drives them is how much of the house is wet, whether the water was clean rain or contaminated ground water, which materials are involved, how many days of drying and which equipment, and how much rebuilding follows. A tree strike also brings tarping and structural repair by other trades, which are separate from the water cleanup.

Insurance and storm water

Rain driven through a storm-damaged roof or window is often treated as sudden damage. Ground water rising from outside is handled differently, and a standard policy usually leaves out flood unless you bought separate coverage. Sump pump failure and sewer backup frequently need their own endorsement. Read the policy, call the insurer, and hold on to photos and receipts. For rebuild permits, contact the Stoneham Building Department on Central Street.

Frequently asked questions

Is emergency storm water damage cleanup available in Stoneham?

Yes. Calls are taken at all hours, in the middle of a storm or on a holiday. A crew goes out the same day when one is open, though no arrival time can be promised. Tell us where the water came in, what is wet and whether power or lines are involved.

Does a sump pump failure count as storm damage?

It can cause the same kind of damage. Whether it is covered depends on your policy, since sump pump overflow often needs its own coverage. Either way, the cleanup is the same: extract the water, remove wet materials and dry the structure.

Can you patch the roof or board up the window?

No. A roofer, window installer or carpenter handles openings. Our side is the restoration crew that pumps out water, pulls wet materials and dries and cleans the house. A tarp or board from another trade will help stop more water coming in.

What if a tree fell on the house?

Keep everyone out of the damaged area and stay away from any wires. Call the utility or 911 for downed lines before the restoration line. Once it is safe, a crew can handle water cleanup, while tree removal and structural work go to other trades.
