A brown stain spreads across a bedroom ceiling in July, and the upstairs air handler is sitting right above it. This is the usual shape of AC water damage: slow, quiet and found late.

Air conditioners make water by design. Warm air crosses a cold coil, and the moisture drips into a pan and drains through a pipe. When that path clogs or cracks, the water goes somewhere else.

How an AC leak starts

An AC unit pulls a surprising amount of water out of summer air, often several gallons on a humid day. That water leaves through a condensate drain line, usually a small PVC or vinyl pipe. Algae and sludge clog it, joints crack, or the line sags and pools. The drain pan under the coil then fills and overflows, or rusts through.

Where the air handler sits changes the damage. In an attic, the leak soaks insulation and then the ceiling drywall below. In a closet or basement, it hits flooring and the wall base. Ductless mini-split indoor units can leak down the wall when the drain pitch is wrong or the line is blocked. Frozen coils are another cause: ice builds up on a restricted system, then melts faster than the pan can handle.

Why the damage shows up late

Condensate leaks are slow. Insulation absorbs water first, so the ceiling looks normal until the insulation is full. Then a stain, a bubble or a sag appears, often far from the unit because water runs along joists and ductwork before it drips. By that point the materials have been damp for some time.

Summer humidity keeps attic air heavy, so wet insulation dries slowly. If the unit runs only a few months a year, a clog can sit unnoticed until the first hot week.

Safety before you touch it

Turn the AC off at the thermostat, then at the unit's switch or breaker, if you can reach it dry. Running a unit that is already leaking adds more water. A sagging, bulging ceiling can hold a lot of trapped water and may collapse, so keep people away from it. Never poke it from directly below.

If water is near light fixtures, outlets or an attic electrical box, stay out of the area. Any electrical risk calls for the utility or 911 before the restoration line. Mold risk at wet insulation

Damp insulation and ceiling drywall in a warm attic are a good place for mold. Growth can begin on damp materials after about 48 hours. If you find dark growth or a heavy musty smell, read about mold remediation. Fix the drain first, since no cleanup lasts while the source is still dripping. Testing and remediation follow the S520 standard, and health questions belong to a physician.

Insurance fit

Insurers often separate sudden and accidental water damage from gradual damage. A condensate line that leaked for weeks may be treated as a maintenance issue, while a pan that cracked overnight may be treated differently. Flood coverage does not apply. Review the policy with your insurer, and hold on to photos and the technician's repair notes.

Frequently asked questions

Why is there a ceiling stain but no dripping water?

Water soaks into attic insulation first, then into the ceiling drywall, which holds it until it reaches its limit. The stain may appear weeks after the leak began. Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera help find the full wet area before anything is cut or painted.

Who fixes the AC unit itself?

An HVAC technician repairs the drain line, clears the clog, replaces a pan or corrects a mini-split drain slope. We do not repair or replace AC equipment. We dry and clean up the water damage. Have the leak stopped first, because drying while water still drips is wasted effort.

Is it safe to leave wet attic insulation in place?

Usually not. Wet insulation holds water against the ceiling and gives mold a place to grow, so it is normally removed and bagged.

Can I run the AC while the ceiling is wet?

Turn it off until the leak is fixed. A running unit with a blocked drain keeps producing water. After the technician clears the line, the drying equipment can work without the leak starting over.

Will homeowners insurance cover an AC leak?

Coverage turns on the policy wording and on whether the leak was abrupt or slow. Many policies treat a slow condensate leak differently from a sudden pan failure. Flood coverage is unrelated. Read your policy, ask your insurer, and keep dated photos and repair records.
